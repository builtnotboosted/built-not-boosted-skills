# Hooks, retention and reactive scripts

Open this when a hook is not landing, when planning a batch so hooks vary, or when scripting a reaction to news or a data release.

## Why the first seconds matter

On every short-form platform the first two or three seconds decide whether a video is watched, and completion is what the algorithms reward. So the hook goes first, in the opening frame, with no warm-up. If your first sentence is throat-clearing, cut it and start on sentence two.

But "first" does not mean "loud". A warm, plainly said line about something that genuinely matters to the viewer holds people better than a shouted gimmick, and it builds the kind of trust that turns viewers into customers.

## Six hook patterns

All of them can be said warmly. Rotate them across a batch.

- **Warning or gotcha:** "Before you sign for a new boiler, there's one line in the quote worth checking." Often the strongest performer. Say it beside the viewer ("before you buy, check this one"), never scolding ("you're making this mistake").
- **Contrarian:** "We're all told to post every day. Sometimes that's the worst thing you can do."
- **Nobody-tells-you:** "Nobody tells you what actually happens to your deposit when a builder goes bust."
- **Just-landed (reactive):** "The new energy price cap just came out, here's what it means for your bill, not the headline."
- **Identity call:** "If you're opening a café in the next six months, watch this before you sign a lease." Pre-qualifies the exact viewer.
- **Numbered:** "Three things your accountant wishes you'd send them in January, and the second one saves the most time." Write the payoff for every numbered item before you write the hook.

What tends to fail on camera: a staged little scene ("You've booked the van for nine. Bold move."), a riddle built on repeated phrases, and clipped one-word questions. People replace them with the plain version, so write the plain version.

## On-screen text hook

Every video gets a 3 to 5 word text hook (3 if you can) shown from the first frame, so it works with the sound off and reads on the grid. It promises without answering: "Gears slipping?", "Before you sign this", "Quote just arrived?" all work. "Check your chain" fails because it is the advice itself. It goes in `edit-notes.txt` as HOOK ON SCREEN.

## The retention shape

Hook, open loop, value, payoff, one ask.

- **Open loop:** the hook implies a question; the intrigue beat makes the viewer feel it; the answer comes in the delivery, not before.
- **Value:** one clear idea, tight. If a line can go, it goes. A change of angle, an on-screen text change or a shift in tone every so often resets attention; these are mostly made in the edit, so the script only needs the natural beats.
- **Midpoint re-hook** on anything over a minute: one plain line that re-earns the waverer by naming the real point ("and this is the one that actually saves you money"). Plain, not a drum-roll.
- **Payoff:** close the loop you opened. If viewers do not get what was promised, they will not watch the next one.
- **One ask after the payoff**, never stacked.

**Record modular where it helps:** a self-contained 30 to 45 second "hook plus one clear answer" can live inside a fuller 2 minute explainer, so the edit can cut both without rewriting.

## Reactive and data-release scripts

When news lands (a price change, a rule change, an industry report, a local story), the job is to translate it, not report it.

1. **Hook on the freshness and the stakes**, not the statistic: "The council just changed the bin collection days, here's what it means if you run a shop on the high street."
2. **Give the number or fact once, plainly**, then leave it. Nobody feels a percentage.
3. **Pivot straight to "what this means for you"**, split by who is watching, using "if you're..." to switch between them ("if you're a tenant... if you own the building..."). Include only the segments the news genuinely moves; a bolt-on segment to cover everyone gets cut on camera anyway.
4. **One next step:** the try-this close with its question.

Speed beats polish: a same-day, slightly rough piece while the story is live beats a polished one two days late. Keep forward-looking comment neutral, do not predict undecided outcomes or take political sides, and do not hard-quote a figure on camera that may change before it airs (put the exact figure in the caption with its source and date). You can quote a named person's actual, on-record words with attribution, and cite officially announced dates.

## Closes that earn engagement

- **Try-this plus question (standard):** one small step doable in a couple of minutes, with a question about that step. "Pull out last month's invoice and find the payment terms. What are yours? Let me know in the comments." Naming the comments ("let me know in the comments") works better spoken than a bare "let me know".
- **Match the question to the topic's own trigger.** A video about losing a big client should ask about that, not about a neighbouring problem like late payment. Check the question against the subject before filing.
- **A helpful offer instead of the question,** occasionally: "If you want the plain-English version for your situation, the link's on my profile, no pressure." It replaces the question, never adds to it.
- **Retired patterns:** comment-keyword asks ("comment GUIDE"), "smash that follow", a long formatted sign-off block on shorts. A quick human goodbye line is fine and usually wanted.
- **Long-form exception:** three asks at the end (try-this, watch-next, one question).

If a piece genuinely has no doable step, a good last line and the question are enough.

## Hooks that stay on the right side of advertising rules

Keep the tension in a real, checkable fact ("this clause could cost you hundreds"), never in a promised outcome ("you'll definitely save", "guaranteed results"). Outcome promises are exactly what advertising and financial regulators flag, and most platforms' ad policies too. Be warm and honest on anything that touches health, money or safety; never scaremonger.
